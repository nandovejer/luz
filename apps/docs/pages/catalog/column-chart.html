How it works

A table of one head row of names and one row of figures. Each figure is the text of its cell, and the cell draws its column under it, from the foot up to --v, in --luz-chart-1, with the track in --luz-chart-track. The columns share the width evenly.

Work out --v as the value over the top of the scale, the same top for every cell. The inline style sets only that. With a strict style-src, set it in a rule of your own, such as .sat { --v: .25 }.

At the default text size, up to seven columns, or three groups of two, fit a 320 px screen. For more, or with larger text, put the table in a luz-table-wrap: it scrolls inside it.

The figure of each cell is above its column and the name below it, as an axis. With several series, each row is a group of columns, --luz-column-chart-gap apart (in the CSS-only chart), and the head row is the legend: each series' name after a dot of its colour.

Inside <luz-chart type="column" interactive>, the script adds a header above the table: a button per series, its name and the sum of its figures, written with the page's language. A sum suits counts and amounts, such as visitors or euros, not rates, shares or averages. The caption becomes the title. The columns lose their track: the day under the pointer or the focus gets a light band, and the figures leave the columns for a readout beside it. The columns keep the scale of their --v when a series is turned off, and the last series shown cannot be turned off. The script writes no word of its own: the names, the figures and the caption are yours.

Without JavaScript

It needs none. Without the stylesheet it reads as a table: a head row of names, then the figures. The interactive chart is, without its script, a plain table: a row per day with every figure, which fits the narrowest screen. A table of a few categories that should be columns with no script takes the class luz-column-chart itself.

Keyboard and ARIA

The plain chart takes no focus. A table keeps its roles, so a screen reader reads each figure with its name. The caption names the table and says the unit. With several series the rows are laid out side by side: write the roles of the table in its markup, as the snippet does, so that Safari with VoiceOver still reads a table.

The interactive chart is a group named by the caption, and the script writes the roles of its table (table, rowgroup, row, columnheader, rowheader and cell). Its buttons are toggle buttons (aria-pressed): Enter or Space turns a series off and on, and the last series shown stays on. Its rows take the focus, one Tab stop, and each row is named by its own cells, from your text: “Apr 1, Desktop 252, Mobile 148”, with every series, shown or not. When the focus is in a row, the arrow keys move to the next or the previous day (Left and Right follow the direction of the page), and Home and End to the first and the last; the keys of a screen reader’s browse mode are its own.

The readout has the name of the day as its title, so the band is never the only sign of where you are; it is hidden from assistive technology. Escape puts it away without moving the pointer or the focus (it comes back on another day). A series turned off loses its columns but keeps its cells in the table, so a screen reader still reads every figure.

Do not hide the table: it is the data. The columns are a picture of it and carry no text of their own.

Design

When to use

Use it to compare a few values that have an order or a name, such as the days of a week or the months of a year, on one scale. With several series, it compares a few series category by category, such as two devices month by month. Use the interactive chart for two or more series over many days, when the totals matter and each day is read one at a time.

When not to use

Do not use the interactive chart when every figure must be seen at once: its figures are in the readout, nor for rates, shares or averages: the total of a series is a sum. Do not use it for one value (use a meter), for long names (use a bar list), or for a trend over many points. Keep a table when exact values matter most.

Do and don't

Writing the content

Name the unit and the period in the caption (“Parcels per day, this week”), keep each name to a word or two, and write the figure with its unit when it is not obvious. Name each series in a word or two: its head cell is the name of its button. The data and the labels are yours: the component only draws them.

  • Focus: the plain chart takes none. In the interactive chart, a button and a row take the focus ring, --luz-focus-ring, 2px: inside a button's edge, and around the group of columns of a row.
  • Target and focus: each button of the interactive header is more than 24 by 24 px (WCAG 2.2, 2.5.8), and the readout is placed beside the day that has the focus and never over it (2.4.11). Both criteria are new in WCAG 2.2 and not yet required by EN 301 549 v3.2.1.

A series turned off has a hollow dot and a lighter name, not only another colour.
